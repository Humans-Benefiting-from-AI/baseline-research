# AI + Orthodoxy: Platform Option Map

*Divergent concepts for a standalone hub, with the monetization path attached to each*
Prepared 6 August 2026 · v1 · Elie Schulman

---

## Assumptions Stated Up Front

- **Entity:** new standalone brand. Not 18Forty, not Cohort Learning Labs. Both remain available as distribution partners and as sources of credibility, but the platform is its own thing with its own name.
- **Hub type and buyer:** unresolved. That is what this document is for.
- **Scope:** Orthodox is the center of gravity, not the boundary. Every concept below works if it is trusted by Modern Orthodox institutions first, with a stated posture toward Haredi and cross-denominational edges rather than a pretense of universality.
- **Access limits:** I could not read `~/Projects/ABOUT_ME/` in this session, so this is written to your stated global rules but not to the voice profile itself.

# Part One: The Decision That Governs Every Other Decision

You asked for two things in one sentence: **the central hub for the conversation**, and **a business selling software to the institutions in that conversation.**

Those are in direct tension, and the tension is not a detail to resolve later. It determines the name, the legal structure, the first hire, and whether the Ohrbit analysis you already wrote is an asset or a liability.

Here is the concrete version. You published a piece establishing that an Orthodox Union product declares precise location, phone number, and sexual orientation as identity-linked data available for advertising, in a posture more permissive than the OU's own three other Torah apps. That piece is the single best proof of what you can do that nobody else in this field can do.

Now try to sell software to the OU.

Every serious version of this platform has to answer that. There are four clean answers, and one dirty one.

### Structure A — Two entities, hard firewall
A 501(c)(3) hub does the editorial, the registry, the audits, the convening. A separate for-profit sells software. Disclosed relationship, separate boards, published conflict policy, and the hub never reviews the product company's category.
*Cost:* the product company loses the single most valuable distribution channel it has. *Benefit:* the hub keeps the only asset that actually compounds.

### Structure B — Cover the builders, sell to the institutions
The hub's subject is **products and their makers**. The revenue comes from **institutions buying operational tooling**. You never review a category you sell into. You can audit Ohrbit all day if what you sell schools is AI policy management, not a Torah app.
*This is the cleanest fit for what you have already demonstrated.*

### Structure C — Member-owned co-op
Institutions pay dues, get a vote, and the software is a member benefit rather than a sale. "Selling to them" becomes "serving them." Slow, credible, and structurally immune to the vendor-capture critique.
*Precedent in the space:* JEDAI is explicitly building as educator-governed nonprofit infrastructure. That position is being claimed right now.

### Structure D — Openly a firm with a house view
Drop the neutrality claim entirely. You are a company with strong opinions that publishes them, like a research-driven investment firm. No pretense of being the commons.
*Fast, honest, and forfeits the word "hub."*

### The dirty answer
Be the hub, sell to the people you cover, and let the sponsorship page quietly decide what gets covered. This is where most Jewish institutional media has landed. It works for about three years and then nobody believes you, including you.

**Nothing below can be evaluated without picking one of these.** I would flag that the strongest concepts in this document cluster around B and C, and that A is a common instinct that usually starves both halves.

# Part Two: The Field, as of Today

Ten facts that constrain the design.

1. **Money is now moving.** The Maimonides Fund ran a national RFP for Judaism-and-AI projects with 12-month grants from $18,000 to $250,000, explicitly naming rabbi/educator tooling, Jewish-content concierges, and historical-voice LLMs as target areas. This is the first real institutional capital in the category.

2. **A neutral-infrastructure position is being claimed.** JEDAI (Jewish Education AI), founded by Dr. Shivi Greenfield, former chief strategy officer of the Jewish Agency, is building an explicitly nonprofit, educator-governed "shared technological grid" for the Jewish world, backed by roughly $1M from the Israeli Innovation Authority. If your instinct is "neutral infrastructure layer for Jewish education," that instinct now has a funded incumbent.

3. **The institutional convening layer exists but is generic.** Prizmah gathered 100 heads of Jewish day schools in Houston in February 2026 with AI strategy on the agenda. JEIC ran AI sessions at its April 2026 Innovators Retreat in Atlanta, including AI-in-chavruta. Neither is an AI-specific body, and neither is Orthodox-specific.

4. **The Haredi world is organizing against, and losing.** In January 2026 Rabbi Elya Ber Wachtfogel convened Hasidic elders and yeshiva heads in Lakewood; the plan included a communal fast day and pushing phones that block AI services. Skver banned AI in 2023, Gur in 2026. Meanwhile *Shomrim* reports AI is penetrating those same communities through filtered systems, with users bringing questions about faith, finance, sexuality, and army service.

5. **The number that should anchor everything: 66% of surveyed Orthodox AI users say they ask AI questions about halacha.** That behavior is already universal, already unsupervised, and has no product built for it that behaves responsibly.

6. **The tool landscape is wide and undifferentiated.** Your own analysis of Sefaria's showcase found 110 projects, of which roughly a dozen of the 21 AI entries are the same product — a chat box over Sefaria. Almost everything is read-only. Almost nothing produces new structured data.

7. **Nobody is watching the products.** Your Ohrbit deep dive is, as far as I can tell, the only serious independent audit of a Jewish AI product that exists. There is no Consumer Reports, no procurement rubric, no seal.

8. **Nobody has measured anything.** No retention data for Daf Yomi in a century. No adoption survey of Orthodox institutions. No benchmark for how the major models handle a halachic question. Whoever publishes first owns the numbers everyone cites.

9. **Commercial vendors are already selling into adjacent Jewish institutions.** Makom Connect launched in July 2026 with AI-powered membership, communications, and fundraising tooling for synagogues. The operational-software land grab has started, and it is starting on the non-Orthodox side.

10. **43% of the largest American Jewish day schools are invisible to AI engines**, per the Ronn Torossian Family Foundation's 2026 Jewish Day School AI Visibility Index. Institutions in this sector are not merely unprepared; most have no idea what their exposure even is.

## The White Space, in One Sentence

**The field has 110 products, real money arriving, and zero infrastructure for judgment — no registry, no rubric, no audit, no measured data, and no institution capable of telling a head of school which of these things is safe to put in front of a child.**

Everyone is building answers. Nobody is building the layer that decides which answers are trustworthy.

# Part Three: Twelve Concepts

Each is a distinct platform, not a feature variant. Read them as competing bets.

---

## 1. The Registry
**"Every Jewish AI tool, what it actually does, and whether it is any good."**

The canonical public directory. Your Sefaria landscape spreadsheet is already v0.5 of this. Each entry gets a maturity rating, a differentiator statement written honestly (including "not meaningfully differentiated"), a data-practices summary, and a last-verified date.

- **Wedge:** the Sefaria showcase is a promotional list with no evaluation. Nobody has to give you permission to build the honest version.
- **Who pays:** not the readers. Institutional procurement subscriptions; paid pre-launch listings review for builders; the registry as lead-gen for everything else.
- **Moat:** verification labor and update cadence. Directories die when they go stale; that is also why they are hard to copy.
- **Fails if:** it becomes a link farm. A registry with no judgment in it is worth nothing.
- **First proof point:** ship 110 entries publicly within 60 days. You already have the raw material.

---

## 2. The Auditor
**"Ohrbit, as a franchise."**

Independent technical and ethical audits of Jewish AI products: data practices, citation integrity, halachic-handling behavior, model provenance. Published on a schedule, with a right of reply.

- **Wedge:** you have already produced the demonstration. That piece is the strongest single credential in this entire document.
- **Who pays:** builders buying a pre-launch audit before a funder or a rav asks. Funders (Maimonides et al.) buying diligence on grantees. Eventually a certification mark.
- **Moat:** reputation for being unbought. Compounds slowly, dies in one bad call.
- **Fails if:** you sell software into the category you audit. See Structure B.
- **First proof point:** three audits in the first six months, one of which is uncomfortable for a large institution.

---

## 3. The Standards Body
**"The model policies every Orthodox institution is about to need and none of them can write."**

Publish adoptable documents: AI-use policy for day schools, disclosure norms for AI-assisted divrei Torah and teshuvos, a religious-data commitment (the one-pager from your Ohrbit piece that nobody in the category has), student-data handling, teacher-workload guidance, a psak-handling standard.

- **Wedge:** every head of school and shul rabbi needs this document, needs it now, and has no idea how to produce it. It is the highest-demand, lowest-supply artifact in the field.
- **Who pays:** implementation and customization; a policy-management SaaS that keeps the policy current as models change; certification of compliant institutions.
- **Moat:** adoption. Once forty schools run your policy, you are the standard, and standards are the stickiest asset in institutional software.
- **Fails if:** written by someone with no halachic standing. This needs a named rabbinic advisory body from day one, not later.
- **First proof point:** one policy, adopted verbatim by three named schools, published with their names on it.

---

## 4. The Council
**"The private room where the people actually deciding this talk to each other."**

An invite-only practitioner network: heads of school, shul rabbis, poskim engaging seriously, Jewish AI builders, funders, a few technologists. Chatham House rules. Annual gathering, quarterly calls, a private channel.

- **Wedge:** the public conversation is performative and the private one does not exist. Everyone in this field currently thinks they are the only one confused.
- **Who pays:** institutional membership dues, sponsorship, the annual event. Software later, as a member benefit rather than a sale (Structure C).
- **Moat:** the roster. Impossible to copy once assembled.
- **Fails if:** it becomes a vendor showcase, or if the Haredi and Modern Orthodox participants can't be in the same room, which is a real risk you should test before announcing.
- **First proof point:** 25 named institutions in a room before you have a website.

---

## 5. The Journal of Record
**"The weekly everyone in Jewish AI reads."**

A newsletter and essay platform. Not another podcast — the podcast lane is 18Forty's and it is well covered by the existing playbook. This is written, argumentative, and reported.

- **Wedge:** there is no publication of record. Coverage is scattered across JTA, eJP, Jewish Insider, Times of Israel, and blogs, none of which employ anyone who understands both the halacha and the retrieval architecture.
- **Who pays:** almost nothing directly. This is not a business. It is the distribution engine that makes 1, 2, 3, and 4 possible.
- **Moat:** voice and cadence.
- **Fails if:** treated as the business rather than the top of the funnel.
- **First proof point:** 1,000 subscribers including 50 heads of school, in 90 days.

---

## 6. The Shailah Layer
**"66% of them are already asking. Build the thing that handles it honestly."**

Not an AI rabbi. The opposite. A product that detects when a question is asking for psak, refuses to answer it, and instead assembles a properly sourced briefing document — the question, the relevant sugyot, the range of positions, what turns on the facts — to bring to an actual rav. Optionally routes to a human on the other end. Logs nothing to advertising, ever.

- **Wedge:** every existing product either pretends to pasken or disclaims in a footer. Nobody has built refusal as a feature.
- **Who pays:** shuls and kiruv organizations as a member service; national organizations as infrastructure; possibly a consumer subscription, though I would not lead there.
- **Moat:** rabbinic endorsement, which is slow and non-transferable. Also the structured question taxonomy you'd accumulate, which is genuinely scarce data.
- **Fails if:** you cannot get a posek of real standing to put his name on the refusal logic. Without that it is a toy.
- **First proof point:** one beis din or one major shul running it as their intake channel.

---

## 7. The School Operating Layer
**"AI policy, detection, and limudei kodesh tooling, in one place."**

The day-school product: policy templates and enforcement, assignment redesign, AI-assisted-work detection that is honest about its limits, Hebrew and limudei kodesh specific tooling, teacher workload relief, parent communication.

- **Wedge:** day schools are the largest institution count with the most acute pain and the least capacity.
- **Who pays:** schools, per-seat or per-building. Also the natural place for Maimonides-style grant capital.
- **Moat:** limudei kodesh specificity. General ed-tech will never build Hebrew decoding, gemara assignment design, or trop practice.
- **Fails if:** JEDAI gets there first with a free, educator-governed, Israeli-Innovation-Authority-subsidized version. **This is the single most contested concept in the document.** Read their positioning carefully before committing.
- **First proof point:** three paying schools, not three pilots.

---

## 8. The Institutional Corpus Layer
**"Your yeshiva has 4,000 shiurim nobody can search. Fix that."**

Private retrieval and agent tooling over an institution's own material: shiur archives, teshuvos, board minutes, newsletters, curricula, alumni records. Hosted, permissioned, no data leaves.

- **Wedge:** boring, unglamorous, immediately valuable, and requires no theological position whatsoever. Every institution has this problem and none of them has named it.
- **Who pays:** yeshivos, publishers, shiur archives (YUTorah, OUTorah, Torah Anytime), national organizations. Real budgets, annual contracts.
- **Moat:** switching cost once the corpus is indexed and the permissions are configured.
- **Fails if:** it commoditizes. This is the concept most likely to be eaten by a general-purpose vendor in 18 months.
- **First proof point:** one large archive under contract, referenceable by name.

---

## 9. The Kosher Access Layer
**"The ban won't hold. Build the thing that holds instead."**

The filtered-AI equivalent of a kosher phone: a rabbinically supervised access layer for communities that have banned open AI but whose members are using it anyway, per *Shomrim*'s reporting.

- **Wedge:** the largest unserved population in the entire space, and the one where the gap between stated policy and actual behavior is widest.
- **Who pays:** communities, filtering organizations, phone distributors. Potentially enormous volume.
- **Moat:** rabbinic authorization, which is the whole product.
- **Fails if:** you are an outsider, which you are. This requires an insider partner with standing in Lakewood or Boro Park, and it requires being willing to be attacked by people who think any access layer is a chillul. **Highest ceiling, highest risk, and the one I would not attempt first.**
- **First proof point:** a single named rav publicly backing a pilot.

---

## 10. The Research Institute
**"Nobody has measured anything. Measure it."**

Original research: an annual Orthodox AI adoption and attitudes survey; a benchmark of how the major models handle halachic questions, scored against known sources; the first-ever retention study of daily learning; a hallucinated-midrash incidence rate.

- **Wedge:** the absence is total. Your Ohrbit piece already documents that no Daf Yomi retention data exists after a century.
- **Who pays:** funders, directly. This is the most fundable concept in the document and the easiest Maimonides letter of intent.
- **Moat:** longitudinal data. Year three is worth ten times year one.
- **Fails if:** it stays academic and never converts into a product or a standard.
- **First proof point:** one survey with n>800 and press pickup in JTA or eJP.

---

## 11. The Training Arm
**"AI literacy for rabbis, teachers, and Jewish nonprofit staff."**

Cohorts, workshops, certifications. Cohort Learning Labs' native motion, pointed at this sector.

- **Wedge:** demand is immediate and the alternative is a generic corporate AI training that has nothing to say about a Rashi.
- **Who pays:** institutions, per cohort. Revenue in 60 days, not 18 months.
- **Moat:** almost none. Anyone can teach a workshop.
- **Fails if:** it becomes the business. It funds the platform; it is not the platform.
- **First proof point:** two paid cohorts, which also generates your first 40 institutional relationships.

---

## 12. The Studio
**"Don't pick one product. Incubate five."**

A build studio plus fiscal sponsor for small Jewish AI builders. Take the grant money, run several bets, keep equity or licensing.

- **Wedge:** the 110 projects in the Sefaria showcase are overwhelmingly solo builders with no distribution, no design, and no institutional relationships. You could supply all three.
- **Who pays:** funders, plus revenue share from the products that work.
- **Moat:** deal flow and distribution.
- **Fails if:** you are subscale. A studio needs a portfolio to survive its failures, and a portfolio needs capital you may not have yet.
- **First proof point:** two builders shipped through the studio with institutional customers attached.

# Part Four: The Three Combinations That Cohere

Individually these are options. In combination, three of them form actual businesses.

### Combination One — **The Standard**
`Registry (1) + Auditor (2) + Standards Body (3)`, monetized through `School Operating Layer (7)` policy tooling and `Training (11)`.

You become the body that decides what "safe" means in this sector, then sell institutions the tooling to comply with the thing you defined. Structure B keeps it clean: you cover products, you sell process.

*This is the combination that best matches the capability you have already demonstrated on paper.* The Ohrbit piece is not a one-off essay; it is a work sample for exactly this job.

### Combination Two — **The Commons**
`Council (4) + Journal (5) + Research (10)`, monetized through membership and `Corpus Layer (8)` sold as member infrastructure.

Slower, more fundable, structurally durable, and the version most likely to still exist in ten years. Structure C. Highest trust ceiling, lowest revenue velocity.

### Combination Three — **The Product Company**
`Shailah Layer (6) + Corpus Layer (8)`, with `Journal (5)` as pure marketing.

Abandon the neutrality claim (Structure D), build the two products with the largest unserved demand, and use writing to generate pipeline. Fastest to meaningful revenue, forfeits the word "hub."

**What does not cohere:** being the Auditor and running the School Operating Layer against the same buyers. Being the Council and the Studio. Being the Standards Body without rabbinic standing. Those combinations look attractive on a whiteboard and collapse on contact with the first uncomfortable phone call.

---

# Part Five: What I Would Want to Know Before Converging

Ordered by how much each answer would move the recommendation.

1. **Whose name goes on the halachic side?** Every concept touching psak, standards, or Haredi communities is worthless without a named posek or a named rabbinic advisory board. Do you have that relationship or do you need to build it? This single answer eliminates or unlocks four of the twelve.

2. **Do you actually want to run a software company?** Concepts 6, 7, and 8 are five-year engineering commitments with support burdens. Concepts 1–5 and 10 are publishing and convening with a much smaller surface. These are different lives.

3. **What is the capital situation?** The Maimonides RFP structure ($18K–$250K, 12 months) fits Research, Registry, and Standards cleanly. It does not fund a product company. If you are bootstrapping, Training first is close to non-negotiable.

4. **Has anyone talked to JEDAI?** They occupy the neutral-infrastructure-for-Jewish-education position with a funded head start. Partner, differentiate, or collide — but decide deliberately rather than by accident.

5. **Can Modern Orthodox and Haredi participation coexist in your room?** Test this before it is announced. If the answer is no, concepts 4 and 9 need to be separate organizations with separate names.

6. **Are you willing to publish something that costs you a customer?** If yes, the Auditor is your strongest asset and Structure B is your architecture. If no, say so now and build Combination Three honestly instead.
