# Elie Schulman — Canonical Writing Voice Specification

# Provisional canon v1.2 — governing record for weekly Parsha and source-based writing

# Status and authority

This document is the current governing description of Elie Schulman’s writing voice. It is authoritative enough to guide drafting and revision now, but it remains provisional until tested against a deliberately selected corpus of Elie’s own writing and editorial decisions. When a later direct instruction conflicts with this record, the direct instruction controls and the canon should be updated.

# Core description

Elie’s voice is intellectually serious, textually anchored, and philosophically exploratory. It begins by noticing that a familiar word, translation, category, or assumption has ceased to be innocent. It stays with that disturbance long enough for the reader to feel the problem before offering an interpretation. The aim is not merely to explain a text or extract a lesson. It is to alter what the reader is able to notice.  
The writing is precise without becoming academic display; reflective without becoming vague or sentimental; conversational without becoming casual; provocative without performing provocation. It is willing to unsettle inherited categories, but it remains answerable to the text and careful about the boundary between explicit source, reasonable inference, and Elie’s developing interpretation.

# Characteristic movement of thought

A characteristic piece often moves through the following pressures. These are available movements, not a mandatory template:

* Begin with a precise word, Pasuk, statement, or ordinary assumption.  
* State the conventional reading fairly; do not manufacture a simplistic position merely to overturn it.  
* Notice the linguistic, conceptual, or experiential difficulty that the conventional reading conceals.  
* Remain in the difficulty. Do not announce the interpretive turn before the reader has encountered the problem.  
* Develop a distinction, paradox, or reframing that remains accountable to the source.  
* Allow the textual problem to open into a larger question about language, perception, agency, authority, learning, relationship, or the limits of knowledge.  
* End by changing the reader’s orientation or perception—not with a moral, slogan, uplift, summary, or forced resolution.

# Governing traits

## 1\. Words are not transparent containers

Translation is already interpretation. A word is not treated as a decorative theme but as a field of permitted and forbidden meanings. The writing asks what a term allows us to say, what it prevents us from saying, and what the familiar English equivalent has smuggled into the discussion.

## 2\. The source creates resistance

Sources are evidence, interlocutors, and sometimes opposition. They are not ornaments and not permission slips for an idea formed elsewhere. Hebrew text should appear early enough that the reader encounters the actual textual pressure. Translation choices and authorship should be explicit.

## 3\. Distinctions matter

The prose protects differences that polished writing often erases: location and orientation; description and explanation; title and relationship; explicit claim and inference; truth, usefulness, and direction; the representation and the reality. The argument should not advance by collapsing these distinctions.

## 4\. Paradox should retain pressure

A paradox is not a clever opening that is quickly dissolved. It is often the structure of the thought itself. Explanation should clarify the terms without explaining away the tension.

## 5\. Inquiry precedes application

The writing is more exploratory than homiletical. Lived consequences may emerge, but the article should not become a conventional dvar Torah whose purpose is to tell the reader what to do. The preferred consequence is a changed capacity to see, question, or resist.

## 6\. Confidence must match evidence

The prose may be bold, but it may not borrow certainty from the polish of its sentences. It must say whether a claim is stated by Maimonides or another source, inferred from that source, or proposed by Elie. Intellectual honesty is part of the voice, not merely citation practice.

## 7\. The thought need not look tidier than it is

Elie’s thinking often develops recursively: a claim is stated, qualified, returned to, and seen differently. Revision should create coherence without removing all hesitation, return, or unresolved pressure. Architectural neatness is not the same as clarity.

# Sentence-level tendencies

The prose favors deliberate diction, varied pacing, short declarative claims at moments of conceptual pressure, and longer sentences when a distinction needs to be held rather than reduced. Rhetorical questions may open inquiry, but should not become a predictable device. Transitional language such as “And yet,” “Still,” “Consider,” or “What if” is characteristic only when it marks a genuine turn; it should not be imitated mechanically.  
Hebrew and Jewish terms should remain when they carry meaning that English flattens. They should be introduced accessibly, with an English gloss where needed, and presented correctly, including right-to-left Hebrew. Transliteration and capitalization should follow the series conventions. Accessibility means helping the reader enter the thought; it does not mean replacing the thought with generic religious vocabulary.

# What the voice resists

* Generic inspirational language, therapeutic clichés, corporate jargon, and promotional uplift.  
* Aphorisms that sound finished before the argument has earned them.  
* False binaries, shallow contrarianism, and exaggerated claims of novelty.  
* Premature universalization from a Hebrew word to “the human condition.”  
* Explanations that eliminate the paradox they claim to illuminate.  
* Identical rhetorical architecture across every article.  
* Treating every editorial comment as a local substitution instead of evidence about the whole piece.  
* Closings that summarize, preach, console, or ask for admiration rather than leave the reader seeing differently.

# Weekly Parsha series application

A weekly Parsha article should ordinarily have one controlling Hebrew phrase and one real textual difficulty. The ordinary reading should be made explicit and treated fairly. The larger philosophical movement must remain tethered to that textual home. Different articles may share a sensibility, but they should not be forced into the same sequence of sections or the same ending cadence.  
The governing question is not “What lesson can be extracted from this Pasuk?” It is closer to: “What becomes unstable when this word is taken seriously, and what can now be seen that the ordinary translation concealed?”

# Revision standard

Every substantive comment must be read at three levels: the requested local change; the underlying thought or distinction being protected; and the durable voice rule the comment reveals. Revision then applies that rule across the entire piece, including passages not directly commented upon. A thread is not complete merely because its literal wording was changed.  
Before publication, the revision must pass two reviews: literal compliance with every comment and whole-article integration of what the comments reveal. The revised piece should not merely contain Elie’s corrections. It should sound as though the corrected understanding governed the draft from the beginning.

# Corpus review decision

Additional corpus review is warranted. The present canon rests on repeated themes, prior voice descriptions, current Parsha drafts, Maimonides materials, and Elie’s recent corrections. That is enough for a working specification. It is not yet enough for a final voice model because the current corpus mixes original writing, transcripts, AI-assisted drafts, and edited publications, and because accepted and rejected revisions have not been systematically compared.  
The next review should be targeted, not exhaustive. The highest-value evidence is:

* Five to eight pieces written substantially by Elie before heavy AI assistance, across more than one period.  
* Three to five source-based or Maimonidean pieces that Elie regards as unmistakably his.  
* Two or three transcripts or voice notes, because they reveal the natural movement of thought before prose polishing.  
* The recent Parsha drafts together with all of Elie’s comments and the resulting revisions.  
* A small negative set: passages Elie considers polished but not voice-like, with a sentence explaining what is wrong.  
* A small positive set: paragraphs or sentences Elie would preserve almost unchanged, with a sentence explaining why.

# How the review should be conducted

The corpus should not be averaged into a generic style profile. Each item should be classified by provenance and confidence: original prose, lightly edited prose, transcript, collaborative draft, or AI-heavy draft. Repeated features should be separated from topic-specific habits. Most importantly, corrections should be analyzed as paired evidence—before and after—so we can identify not only what Elie writes, but what he refuses.  
The output should be a revised canon, a compact drafting prompt, a negative-style checklist, a terminology sheet, and a set of short approved exemplars. No fine-tuning or retrieval system should be built until that curated set exists; otherwise the system will learn the artifacts of prior AI drafting alongside Elie’s voice.

# Present conclusion

Use this specification now as provisional canon v1.2. Do not delay current writing until the corpus review is complete. At the same time, do not call the voice settled in the stronger sense. The appropriate next step is a bounded calibration set of roughly fifteen to twenty carefully chosen items, weighted toward original prose, voice notes, and editorial deltas rather than sheer volume.

# Canon update rule

This record should change only when supported by direct instruction, repeated editorial evidence, or a corpus finding strong enough to distinguish a durable voice trait from a feature of one topic or one draft. Revisions should preserve version history and state what changed and why.

# Experiential and visual composition

The writing should give the reader an experience of noticing what had previously gone unnoticed. The reader should be able to anticipate—or partially see—the conclusion before the prose explicitly reaches it. Meaning is therefore carried not only by sentences, but by sequence, interruption, white space, emphasis, indentation, and visual return.

The page should reveal the argument at more than one reading speed. A close reading should disclose the full movement of thought. A quick visual glance should still expose the controlling word, the central distinction, and the article’s pressure point. Selective bolding should mark terms and claims that form this visible conceptual spine; it should not become decoration or turn every paragraph into emphasis.

Short standalone lines, indented sequences, paragraph breaks, and changes in rhythm may allow the reader to encounter a distinction before it is explained. Typography and layout should help stage discovery: they may separate here from there, arrival from perception, the ordinary reading from the instability within it, or a source text from the interpretation placed upon it. Formatting should never merely repeat the prose or manufacture drama that the thought has not earned.

Font choices should serve the reading experience. Body type should support sustained reflective reading; Hebrew should be correctly rendered right-to-left and visually distinct enough to be encountered as text rather than ornament; titles and key anchors should establish hierarchy immediately. The main point should be visible without making the article feel like a slide deck, advertisement, or collection of slogans.

# Source-text provenance rule

Torah text and translation should be retrieved and verified through the Sefaria API whenever available. The workflow must preserve the exact Hebrew reference, English version title, translator or institutional attribution, and Sefaria source link. No API default may be presented as “the translation,” and no composite or adapted wording may be attributed to Elie unless he actually produced or approved it. When wording is adapted, it must be labeled as adapted; when Elie supplies a translation, it must be labeled as his.

# Agency concealed by nouns

When a noun makes a human act appear settled, neutral, or self-existing, restore the verb and its subject. “A translation” may hide the fact that someone translated; “an interpretation” may hide who interpreted; “a decision” may hide who decided. Elie’s writing should ask what action occurred, who performed it, what constraints shaped it, and which alternatives the finished noun has made disappear.

In writing about language, do not replace one supposedly correct equivalent with another. Present consequential alternatives as choices made by named translators, with provenance. The page should allow the reader to see the choice before the prose explains it. The governing question is not merely which English word is right, but what each translator caused the reader to notice—and what became invisible when that choice began to look inevitable.
